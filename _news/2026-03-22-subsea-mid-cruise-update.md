The SUBSEA expedition continues across the South Atlantic Subtropical Gyre, with the past several days marked by intensive round-the-clock science operations. The team recently returned to the location of the original sampling station for an additional 96-hour occupation designed to capture detailed observations of particle export, upper ocean productivity, and nutrient cycling.

During the station occupation, researchers deployed a full sediment trap array spanning five depths throughout the upper 300 meters of the ocean to investigate how particle flux changes with depth. The team also deployed the Wirewalker, a free-drifting platform that repeatedly profiles the upper ocean while measuring temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, fluorescence, and particle abundance. Together with the Seaglider already operating in the region, these autonomous systems are providing an unprecedented view of upper ocean biogeochemistry and ecosystem variability.

Science operations during these long stations are continuous and highly coordinated. Researchers have now completed more than 40 CTD casts during the cruise, alongside trace metal rosette deployments, in situ pump operations filtering hundreds of liters of seawater, productivity and nitrogen fixation measurements, day and night zooplankton net tows, shipboard incubation experiments, and optical measurements that characterize how light penetrates through the water column.

As the expedition continued northeastward, the team conducted underway CTD profiling and near-surface nutrient measurements while transiting toward a region where satellite observations indicated elevated chlorophyll concentrations persisting for several months. Researchers also completed additional short-duration stations for One-OBVI observations, trace metal sampling, and experiments.

The team recently arrived at Station 7, the site of the next long-duration occupation. Using near-real-time underway CTD observations, researchers identified this location as an area where upper ocean chlorophyll concentrations were elevated relative to surrounding waters. Initial observations suggest strong particle abundance in the upper ocean, with the chlorophyll maximum occurring at shallower depths than at previous stations.

Throughout the expedition, researchers have also been tracking diel vertical migration using the ship’s acoustic instruments. Zooplankton and small fish ascend hundreds of meters toward the surface each evening to feed before returning to deeper waters before sunrise. These migrations play an important role in transporting carbon and nutrients between the surface ocean and the twilight zone.

The SUBSEA team continues to work around the clock with support from the captain, crew, and marine technicians aboard the R/V Falkor (too). Following completion of the current station occupation, the expedition will continue northward toward waters characterized by very low chlorophyll concentrations, where researchers will conduct one final long-duration station occupation.
