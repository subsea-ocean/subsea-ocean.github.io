The SUBSEA expedition continues across the subtropical South Atlantic, with the team recently completing operations at Station 7, the second of the expedition’s three 96-hour stations. Station 7 was selected based on satellite observations indicating elevated chlorophyll concentrations in the near-surface ocean that had persisted for several months. Upon arrival, researchers encountered what appeared to be a decaying diatom bloom, including large aggregates of the diatom *Rhizoselenia*.

At Station 7, the chlorophyll maximum occurred at relatively shallow depths, ranging from approximately 60–85 meters. Filtering seawater samples proved slow due to the elevated biomass in the upper ocean, and visual inspection of sediment trap material from shallower depths suggested enhanced particulate export from the surface ocean.

Science operations at Station 7 included the full suite of autonomous and shipboard measurements conducted throughout the expedition. The team deployed sediment traps, the Seaglider, the Wirewalker, and productivity arrays, while also conducting numerous CTD casts, trace metal rosette deployments, turbulence measurements using the VMP profiler, McLane pump operations, and zooplankton net tows.

Researchers also conducted a wide range of experiments examining nutrient limitation of phytoplankton growth, nitrogen uptake and remineralization, and microbial decomposition of zooplankton fecal pellets. These collaborative experiments are helping scientists better understand the biological and chemical processes controlling carbon and nutrient cycling in the subtropical gyre.

After completing operations at Station 7 on March 25, the expedition began transiting northward toward Station 10, the final 96-hour station occupation of the cruise. Along the transit, the team conducted additional short-duration stations for One-OBVI observations, trace metal sampling, and experimental water collection. Researchers also continued round-the-clock underway CTD profiling every two hours while measuring near-surface phosphate concentrations across the region.

The team has now arrived at Station 10, located in a region characterized by persistently low chlorophyll concentrations based on satellite ocean color observations. Initial measurements indicate extremely clear blue waters, with the chlorophyll maximum occurring much deeper than at previous stations, reaching depths near 150–170 meters.

Operations at Station 10 are already underway and include deployments of sediment traps, the Seaglider, and the Wirewalker, followed by evening CTD casts and McLane pump deployments extending late into the night. These final long-duration stations remain intensive but highly productive, allowing the team to compare ecosystem structure and nutrient cycling across dramatically different regions of the South Atlantic Subtropical Gyre.

As the cruise approaches its final phase, researchers are also beginning preparations for arrival in Salvador, including organizing sample shipments and coordinating transport logistics for the large volume of scientific samples collected during the expedition.

The expedition has also been documented extensively through photography and video. Science communicator Bernie Descloux recently released the first video update from the cruise, highlighting daily life and science operations aboard the R/V Falkor (too).
